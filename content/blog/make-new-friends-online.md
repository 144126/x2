Making friends as an adult is genuinely harder than it was at any earlier point in your life, and it's not because something is wrong with you. School, college, and early jobs all come with built-in, repeated exposure to the same people — the single biggest predictor of friendship formation. Once that structure disappears (you graduate, you move, you go remote, you have a kid), the exposure disappears with it, and "just meet people naturally" stops being realistic advice.

Here's what actually works instead.

## 1. Shared interest beats proximity

The old model of friendship — you're just around the same people enough times — doesn't require anything in common beyond geography. Online, you don't get geography for free, so you need to replace it with something else: shared interest. Talking to someone who's into the exact same niche thing you are skips the slow small-talk phase almost entirely, because you already have something to talk about on message one.

This is the single biggest lever available to you, and it's why "join a random chat app and hope" underperforms "find a room/group built around something you actually care about."

## 2. Join — or start — a chat group around a specific hobby

Not "gaming." Not "movies." Specific: the exact roguelike you're stuck on, the specific era of movies you rewatch, the niche coffee-brewing method you're obsessed with. Specific groups have less noise and higher-quality conversation, because everyone in them opted in for the same narrow reason.

If a group like that doesn't already exist where you're looking, start one. The bar is lower than it feels — a short description of what the group is about and a couple of tags is enough for the right people to find it later, and you don't need anyone else to show up on day one to make it worth starting.

## 3. Use apps built for friendship, not apps built for dating

This sounds obvious but it's the most common mistake. Dating apps optimize for a completely different signal (romantic/physical interest) and the entire interaction model — swiping on photos, one match at a time — is built around that, not around finding people to be friends with. If you use a dating app to try to make friends, you're fighting the product's own design the whole time. Look specifically for apps built around interests and communities, not profiles and swiping.

## 4. Get on a call before you decide it's not working

Text is a low-commitment way to start, but a lot of potential friendships stall out in text because tone doesn't translate and momentum dies between replies. If a text conversation is going fine but not great, a single voice or video call often resolves in five minutes what would've taken two weeks of back-and-forth messaging to figure out — namely, whether you actually click.

## 5. Show up consistently, not just once

This one has nothing to do with any app. Friendship is built by repeated low-stakes contact over time, the same mechanism that made your school friendships work in the first place. If you found a good group or a good person, the move isn't one great conversation — it's showing up again next week.

## What actually separates a good "make friends" app from a bad one

- **Interest-first, not looks-first.** You should be able to search or browse by what you're into, not just see a stack of faces.
- **A group option**, so you're not required to carry an entire 1:1 conversation with a stranger from a cold start.
- **No pressure to build a performative profile** before anyone will talk to you.
- **Low friction to try it** — if it takes fifteen minutes of setup before you can talk to a single person, most people never get past setup.
